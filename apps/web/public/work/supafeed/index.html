Backstage

Everyone I know who uses an AI agent has the same problem. Their agent is good at finding things: a concert on Thursday, a job that fits, a bakery worth the walk. Then it pastes the find into a chat, where it scrolls away with everything else.

By September 2026, agents like OpenClaw (which runs on your own computer), Hermes and Meta's Muse could browse the web and work on a schedule. What they didn't have was a place to put what they found. So the finding happened, and then the finding got lost.

Supafeed is that place. Your own agent fills a private feed, a plan for your day and small dashboards. You read them on your phone, and talk back to the agent from the same app.

Where I came in

This started as something else. ClawParty, which a collaborator and I started in February, was a pre-networking platform: AI agents met each other on their humans' behalf before events. Agents wrote plenty. Nobody, human or agent, needed to read much of it.

In September I took it over alone and turned it inside out: the agent's posts go to one reader, the person who owns the agent. Everything from that point is my design and my build. I wrote the plans, made the product calls, and directed Claude Code and Codex to write most of the code, checking what shipped on my phone and in the simulator.

My contributions

  • Research: a scan of 9 product families the day before building, then a review of 7 reference products and an inventory of the 41 needs I'd stated as the first user.
  • The core model: 17 typed card kinds that agents fill and the app draws.

What was the problem?

People's finds were scattered across chats, and their agents had nowhere better to put them.

I was the first user, so I started with myself: every need I'd stated, collected into one list of 41. They ran from "replace morning scrolling with morning strolling" to "feed noise must not pollute the user's wiki or memories." Three themes kept coming up:

  1. A chat is the wrong shape for a find. An event needs a date, a place, a price and a reminder. A chat message has text.
  2. The agent has no screen of its own. It can only talk. It can't lay out a card, so every find arrives looking like every other message.
  3. Neither side can see the other's work. I couldn't tell what my agent was looking for, how often, or whether it had stopped. It couldn't tell what I'd saved, dismissed or wanted more of.

The third one turned out to be the real problem. Two parties were working on the same thing with no shared surface.

What research gave me

Before drawing anything I wanted to know whether this already existed. I scanned nine product families: read-it-later apps (Readwise Reader), feed readers (Feedly, Inoreader), bookmark tools (Karakeep, mymind), algorithmic feeds (Bluesky custom feeds, YouTube) and AI news apps, including Artifact, which its founders shut down in 2024.

I compared them on who does the finding, who owns the result, and where it ends up. Most answered you find it yourself or a platform's algorithm finds it for everyone. A couple, like Karakeep and Readwise, already let agents in through an API, so agent access alone couldn't be the difference. None was built around your own agent finding events, places and videos and handing them to a feed you own.

Then I studied seven products closer and took one lesson from each:

ReferenceWhat I took
Readwise ReaderFinite daily editions with a real "you're caught up" state
KarakeepAgent APIs and tags are table stakes now, so they can't be the differentiator
PuddleTurn inspiration into something you can act on: places, times, bookings
LaterollPrivate, mixed-media rediscovery is appealing
Perplexity, ManusShow the schedule, the run history and what happens next
Linear's agentsAn agent's work has visible states: waiting, working, needs you, done
LakebedOne clear entry command, limits up front, docs that unfold as you need them

What fell out of it. The person needs a calm, finite feed of things they can act on. The agent needs a contract it can fill without guessing. And the space between them needs one rule: show only what actually happened.

Testing my plan without a team

I didn't have colleagues to poke holes in my plans, so I had a second AI agent attack each major plan before it was built. The first review listed eight things to change before coding. Three of them:

  • my setup flow pasted a permanent API key into the chat history, breaking my own rule about keys;
  • a sharing feature would have exposed which private item and research topic a public post came from;
  • my plan promised that old items expire, then put off building the job that clears them.

Not every fix landed on time. Key-free pairing replaced the pasted key. A switch that auto-shared a whole hashtag shipped anyway, and I took it out four days later: now a card goes public only when you share that one card. The review's sharpest line became the rule behind that: a tag an agent adds is a label, not permission to publish.

Design philosophy

I wanted Supafeed to feel like a good assistant's desk: what they found for you, laid out cleanly, with a note on why, and nothing pretending to be done when it isn't.

Not a social feed that wants your attention. Not a chatbot that wants a conversation. An assistant that says "here are four things worth your evening," then stops. That became five rules every screen follows:

01
Agents send facts. The app draws.The agent never decides what a card looks like.
02
Unknown stays unknown.No invented dates, prices, hours or ratings. A missing field shows nothing.
03
Never a fake green.Status comes from what the server saw, not what the agent claims.
04
Private by default.Only you can share a card, and only one at a time.
05
An honest path for every agent.The app says plainly what works with your agent today.

Feature 1/4

Typed cards

A find is not a message. A concert has a date, a venue, a price and a ticket link. A stock story has a ticker and a live price. Paste them into a chat and they all look the same.

What I explored. In September 2026 there were several ways to let an AI agent show something on a screen. I compared them on one question: who draws the interface, the agent or the app?

ApproachThe agent sendsWhy not
AG-UIA live stream of interface eventsNeeds a live session. An agent posting at 6 AM has nobody watching.
A2UIA layout treeStores layout, not facts. A feed can't sort or filter a layout.
MCP AppsMini-apps inside a chatLives inside the chat app, not in a feed.
OpenClaw CanvasA widget the agent writesAgent-written HTML: unsafe, and every card would look different.
Typed cardsFacts, in a fixed shape per kindThe app draws them, sorts them and can restyle them all at once.

Each kind has a short contract: what's required, what's optional and what must never be guessed. An event needs its timing and either a venue or "virtual." A movie card carries IMDb and Rotten Tomatoes scores, and MyAnimeList for anime. The server checks every card against the same contract the agent reads.

The hardest call was what the agent is not allowed to send

Agents love to fill fields. So the contract says no in three places:

  • No prices on stock cards. An agent's price is stale in a minute. Supafeed fetches the live quote itself.
  • No guesses. An unannounced date reads "Date not announced." A missing field shows nothing at all, because a wrong field misleads and a missing one doesn't.
  • No layout. The agent can't add a button, a colour or a section.

The most consequential decision: keep three sources of truth apart

A card holds facts from the agent and facts the server looked up (a venue's address, a movie's scores), and the model keeps a separate layer for your own edits. They're never merged. When the server finds a different venue than the agent posted, it doesn't overwrite; the card shows "Source says…" so you can see both. You always know who said what.

A note only you see. Each card can carry a one-line "why" from your agent, like "You saved two modular synth videos this week." It stays private even if you share the card.
Feature 2/4

Onboarding in one sentence

Most apps onboard a person. Supafeed has to onboard a person and an agent, and the agent does most of the setup. I didn't want anyone copying API keys, and I didn't want a long form. The agent already knows its person, so the app should let it do the talking.

When your user is an agent, the docs are the interface

agents.md is written for a model, not a developer. It tells the agent to interview its person about purpose, not topics: "AI" is a topic; "interface ideas I can use in my own product" is an assignment. It asks the agent to say the plan back before starting, and to offer a schedule only after the first batch lands.

Agents were my test users

I ran real agents through the flow and watched where they broke. Each break became a fix:

  • My own OpenClaw failed its very first status check. Cloudflare blocked its default user agent. An hour later the docs told every agent to send a proper one.
  • Muse opened the approval link in an in-app browser that lost the code after sign-in. Now agents always give the code as well as the link, and there's a page where you can type it.

The docs changed 28 times in 13 days. Each change is versioned, so an agent working from older instructions is told when to read them again.

One question, different paths

Agents connect differently. OpenClaw chats through a small connector running beside it (so far, only on my own Mac). Muse lives in a cloud machine that nothing can call into, so it's designed to check in about once a minute through a bridge it builds itself; that bridge isn't running yet. So the intro asks one question first, "Which agent do you use?", and each answer leads to the right setup message and an honest status page.

Feature 3/4

Edit discovery

A week in, I couldn't remember on my phone what I'd asked my agent to look for. Was it still hunting for hackathons? How often? Could I pause the one about synths? The website had a brief editor from day one; the iPhone didn't.

Each agent keeps a brief: the things it looks for, how often, how many cards each time, and an optional end date. You and the agent edit the same brief. A slide-over on the Feed opens Edit discovery, which shows every task in plain words ("Once a day around 7 PM · 1 card"). Tap one to change how often, pause it, or stop it.

The hardest call: the app can't run the schedule

The agent's clock lives on the agent's own machine, so the app can't change it directly. When you change a task's timing, the app marks the schedule out of date. The next time the agent checks in, it's told to update its own job. The website's editor says so in one line, so nobody thinks a change took effect when it hasn't yet.

Never a fake green

An agent saying "I've set up a daily job" proves nothing: a test run can work while the unattended job lacks a permission it needs. So an agent's status comes from what the server has seen, in seven states from "Waiting" to "Collecting," and it reads "Scheduled" until one of its scheduled runs actually finishes.

Feature 4/4

Talk to your agent

A feed is one-way. Sometimes you want something now: "find me a quiet bar near the venue."

The ✦ button in the tab bar is always there. Tap it to type; hold it to talk; let go to send. Your agent answers in a chat that shows the same native cards as the feed, plus questions with buttons, forms, permission requests and live progress while it works.

The bar I built twice in one day

iOS 26's tab bar can't open into a multi-line message box, so I drew my own Liquid Glass bar. The first version broke scrolling and was reverted the same day. The second is built to measurements I read from the running system bar, with nothing estimated. That Saturday, ten commits touched that one file.

Learning a gesture rule the hard way

Hold-to-talk kept dying a second in, while my finger was still down, and sent nothing. I added a trace to the bar and found the cause: 300 ms into a hold, the app switched to the chat tab, which rebuilt the bar, which removed the button under my finger, so iOS cancelled the touch.

hold began source=agentButton hold listening +300ms tab → ask bar rebuilt under the finger press released lifted=false ← iOS took the touch away

Reconstructed from the bar's trace. "released lifted=false" is the line quoted in the fix.

The rule that came out of it: never move, rebuild or reshape the view under a finger. Show feedback beside it, and navigate on release.

Now a panel appears above the bar while you hold, and the tab changes only when you let go.

"Ready" means proven

"Connected" used to mean the agent's key worked. That isn't chat. Now "Chat ready" means a round trip: the server sends a one-time check, the agent's connector answers only after reaching the agent's actual session, and only then does the app say ready. An agent that polls, like Muse, never shows "ready." It shows when it last checked in.

The system is mostly rules:

  • Cobalt is the one action colour. Peach is for artwork only, never a background; as text it fails contrast (1.39:1 on ivory).
  • Glass for controls, solid for content. Bars and buttons are Liquid Glass; cards are opaque so photos and prices stay readable.
  • One component per card kind, with one shared header (kind, agent, time) so you always know what you're looking at and who found it.
  • Plain words. It's called the Feed, not "Finds." Items are cards. Labels say what something is doing, in the words you'd use.

What this project taught me

I set out to design an app for a person. I ended up designing for two users with opposite needs: a person who wants calm, finished things, and an agent that needs exact rules and honest feedback.

My best decisions were about what not to show: no guessed facts, no fake "connected," no layout from the agent. The more capable the agent, the more the interface has to be the part that refuses to pretend.

Design the boundary, not just the screen.

This is early. One household uses it (mine), and it has no customers yet. One designer built it in 13 days by directing coding agents; that's how it got this far this fast, and the product calls were mine. OpenClaw chat only works where its connector is installed, which today means my Mac. Muse's chat is designed and our half of it is live, but Muse hasn't built its half yet.

What I'd do differently: put steering on the phone in the first week. The research said to make the agent's plan visible, yet the iPhone could show the agent's posts 12 days before it let you steer what the agent looks for. And I'd put the app in front of five other people before the brand got its third name.
